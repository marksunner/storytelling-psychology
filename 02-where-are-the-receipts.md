# Where Are the Receipts?

*A small piece of mental equipment for telling the real thing from the chaff.*

---

Someone owes you two hundred pounds. They swear they paid it back. They remember the day, they remember the handover, and they are a little wounded that you would even raise it. And you, without a flicker of guilt, say: show me. A bank record, a message, a note in a diary, anything. You are not being cruel and you are not calling them a liar. You are asking for the receipt, because somewhere along the way you learned that sincerity and truth are two different things that often travel together and sometimes do not.

You do this all day, and you are good at it. When a mechanic says the gearbox is gone, you want to see the old one, or you get a second opinion. When a builder's final bill is double the quote, you ask for it itemised. When a headline about your own street sounds off, you walk down and look. In the ordinary business of your life you are a competent, instinctive auditor, and you would be insulted to be told otherwise.

And then something odd happens. You open the news, or a feed, or you listen to someone fluent and confident explain how the world really works, and the auditor quietly goes to sleep. The part of you that would never accept "I definitely paid you back" without proof will accept "studies show," "experts agree," or "everyone knows" without asking a single one of the questions you would ask about a two hundred pound debt.

This essay is about waking that auditor up and pointing it at the places it has learned to look away from.

## The auditor switches off exactly where it is needed

Here is the uncomfortable pattern. Your scrutiny does not fade at random. It fades most reliably at the precise moment a claim arrives already agreeing with you. When something confirms what you hoped was true, or flatters the side you already stand on, or completes a story you were enjoying, the demand for a receipt feels almost rude, like doubting a friend. So you wave it through. The claims that most need checking are the ones that feel least like they need it, because feeling right is exactly the sensation a well-built falsehood is engineered to produce. Truth and a satisfying story feel identical from the inside. Only the receipt can tell them apart.

That is not a flaw peculiar to other people. It is the standard equipment all of us are running, and the only question is whether you know it is running.

## The empty hand held up as a full one

Now, the people who want to move you are not stupid, and they have noticed that "where are the receipts" is catching on. So the sophisticated ones no longer skip the receipt. They fake its presence. This is the move worth learning to see, because it is everywhere once you can.

It looks like a citation to a study, offered so confidently that you never notice no study is actually linked, and that when you finally find it, it says something quieter or different. It looks like a number with no denominator: something is up forty per cent, but of what, since when, and measured how. It looks like a screenshot with no source, a quote with no date, "sources say" with no sources. And in its grandest form it looks like a document so thick that its sheer weight feels like proof, pages and pages of it, most of them redacted or beside the point, presented with great ceremony so that the theatre of showing is mistaken for the substance of proof.

That is chaff. It is not evidence. It is the shape of evidence, manufactured to satisfy the auditor without feeding it anything. The empty hand, held up as though it were full, betting you will admire the gesture and not look in the palm.

And here is why the bet is usually a good one. Producing chaff is almost free, and refuting it is enormously expensive. It has a name, Brandolini's law, the bullshit asymmetry principle: the effort needed to refute nonsense is an order of magnitude greater than the effort needed to produce it. One confident sentence can take a careful person a week to dismantle properly, by which time the parade has moved three towns on and nobody is looking back. So the flood is not laziness or accident. It is a strategy, and it works on intelligent people precisely because they are too busy to audit everything and too trusting to audit the things that please them.

## When the missing receipt is sold to you as proof

There is one trap left, and it is the cleverest, so watch for it. When you finally ask for the receipt and none appears, the tempting conclusion is handed to you ready-made: the very fact that it is hidden proves how damning it must be. The redactions become the evidence. The silence becomes the smoking gun. Absence gets sold to you as presence.

Sometimes things really are hidden, of course, so you cannot simply reverse the rule and treat every gap as innocent. What you need instead is a single clean discriminator, and it is this. A real receipt survives being handed to someone who wants you to be wrong. It can be checked by a person on neither side, and it stays standing when they check it. Chaff cannot. Chaff needs you to be friendly, hurried, or already convinced. So the test is not "am I being shown a lot," nor "is something being withheld." The test is: would this survive an unfriendly audit by someone with no reason to spare my feelings? If it only holds up among people who already agree, it was never a receipt. It was a flag you were invited to salute.

## The test that actually costs something

Everything above is easy to aim at your opponents, and that is the final trap, the one that swallows the whole tool. Anyone can demand receipts from the other side. Doing so feels like rigour and is often just tribalism wearing rigour's coat. The demand for evidence, applied only outward, is not scepticism. It is a more sophisticated way of being certain.

So here is the one move that turns this from a weapon into an instrument, and it is genuinely uncomfortable. Take the belief you would least like to be wrong about. The one where the question itself feels slightly offensive. Now ask where its receipts are, and watch what you do. If you find yourself reaching for the story instead of the evidence, reaching for how obviously true it is, how everyone sensible knows it, how only a bad person would ask, then you have found the exact place your auditor is asleep, and it is not on the other side of the room. It is in your own chair.

That flinch is the most valuable signal you will ever get. Not because your cherished belief is necessarily false, but because a belief you cannot bear to audit is being held for reasons that have nothing to do with whether it is true, and that is the condition every propagandist in history has relied on. The person who can ask for a receipt on their own side, and sit with the answer, is already standing outside the story. The person who only ever asks it of their enemies never left.

## The equipment

You do not need to become a permanent sceptic, corroding every pleasure and unable to enjoy a film or trust a friend. You need a reflex, switched on at the few moments that matter, when someone is asking you to believe, to fear, to buy, or to vote. And it fits in four questions.

Am I being shown the thing itself, or only a description of it? Would this survive an unfriendly audit by someone who wants me to be wrong? Is a missing receipt being sold to me as proof of a cover-up? And the one that costs something: does a missing receipt on my own side move me, or do I only ask this of the other?

That is the whole kit. It is small enough to carry and stubborn enough to grow, and once it is planted it tends to run on its own, because the first time you catch a piece of chaff you had been about to swallow, the habit fertilises itself. A story asks only to be believed. A receipt asks to be checked. Learn, at the moments that matter, to tell which one you are holding.
